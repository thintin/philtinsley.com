Adopted is not the same as trusted: the handover test for care systems

Phil Tinsley · November 2026

Most care providers judge a new system by whether staff use it. Logins are up, visits are recorded, care notes are completed on time. On those measures the rollout is a success.

There is a harder test, and it happens at every shift change. Does the next shift trust what is in the record enough to act on it?

When the record is complete but not believed

A system can be fully adopted and still fail at handover. The signs are familiar across the sector: someone on the incoming shift rings the colleague who has just left to check what happened, a paper note is kept “just in case”, or a question about someone’s care is answered by asking around rather than by looking at the record.

None of this shows up on a usage report. Every field may be filled in. But if people check the record by phone or on paper before they act on it, the system has added a step rather than replaced one. Staff are now doing the old job and the new one.

Why trust breaks down

Distrust of a record is usually rational. It tends to come from a small number of causes:

  • Notes written at the end of a shift from memory, rather than at the time
  • Entries that are complete but vague, such as “settled day”, with nothing about what changed
  • Gaps where something was handed over verbally and never written down
  • An occasion where the record turned out to be wrong, which people remember for a long time

Once staff learn that the record cannot always be relied on, they start checking everything. That habit spreads quickly and is slow to reverse.

Why it matters beyond efficiency

Regulation 17 of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 requires providers to keep an accurate, complete and contemporaneous record for each person using the service. If staff do not trust a record enough to act on it, that is a strong sign it may not be meeting that standard.

There is a safety point too. Handover is where information is most easily lost. If the real handover happens in a phone call that nobody writes down, the record and the reality have drifted apart, and nobody can show afterwards what was known and when.

Measuring the right thing

Logins and completed fields tell you whether a system is being used. To know whether it is trusted, look at what happens around handover:

  • How often does the incoming shift contact the outgoing one to check something that should already be in the record?
  • Are paper notes or messaging groups still running alongside the system?
  • When a manager asks a question about someone’s care, can it be answered from the record alone?
  • When an entry is found to be wrong, is it corrected, and is the reason fed back to the team?

None of this is difficult to observe. Watching a few handovers, and paying attention to what people actually do rather than what the system reports, will tell you a great deal.

So here is the challenge. Pick one shift change this week and count how many times someone checks the record by asking a person instead. If the number is more than zero, ask why, and start there.
