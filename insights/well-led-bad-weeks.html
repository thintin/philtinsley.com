Well-led is proved in the bad weeks, not the good days

Phil Tinsley · September 2026

Most care services look well-led on a quiet Tuesday. The rota is full, the manager is in, the phones are calm and the paperwork is up to date. That tells you very little. The real test of leadership comes in the week when two staff call in sick, a safeguarding concern lands and something in the building breaks, all at once.

A system or a person

Every service runs on an operating model, whether anyone has written it down or not. In some services that model is a system: clear routines, shared information and decisions that do not depend on who is on shift. In others, the model is a person. Usually it is the registered manager, sometimes a long-serving deputy or senior carer, who holds the rota in their head, knows which family is waiting for a call back and remembers which audit is overdue.

On a good day you cannot tell the difference. Both look calm and competent. A bad week shows you which one you have, because a person can only be in one place at a time and a system does not need to be.

The fortnight test

A simple way to find out is to ask a direct question: what would happen if your manager was away for two weeks with no phone contact?

Walk it through honestly. Who would build next week’s rota? Who would know which supervisions are due, which medicines audit is outstanding or which safeguarding referral is waiting on a reply? Where would staff take a concern at eight in the evening? If the answer to most of these is “we’d ring them anyway”, the service may be well run, but it is not yet well-led in the sense that matters.

This matters for regulation as well as resilience. CQC is moving towards sector-specific assessment frameworks, but well-led remains one of its five key questions, and Regulation 17 still requires providers to operate effective governance systems. Governance that lives in one person’s memory is hard to evidence and harder to sustain.

Building for the bad week without adding bureaucracy

The answer is not more policies. Most services already have plenty. The aim is to make essential knowledge visible and shared, so the service keeps its shape when key people are stretched or absent. A few practical starting points:

  • Name your critical tasks: list the handful of things that must happen every week regardless of who is in, and name a backup for each.
  • Make the status visible: keep one place, whether a whiteboard or a shared document, that shows what is overdue, open or waiting.
  • Give seniors real authority: be clear about which decisions a deputy or senior carer can make without checking first.
  • Rehearse the gap: treat planned leave as a live test, then review what got stuck and fix it.

None of this needs new software or a new form. It needs someone to decide that the service should not depend on any single person being available.

Your challenge this month

Pick an ordinary week and ask your manager to step back for two days. Not a disappearance, just a deliberate pause. Note every question that still finds its way to them and every task that stalls. That list is your governance gap, and it is the most honest picture you will get of how well-led your service really is.
